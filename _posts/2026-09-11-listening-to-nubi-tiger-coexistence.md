Tiger conservation in Bhutan is multifaceted. It is backed by tradition and culture, strengthened by science and monitoring, sustained by community acceptance, guided by inclusive policies, and enabled through partnerships and international support.

Together, these factors have helped Bhutan remain one of the world's important tiger landscapes. But conservation success also reminds us of something fundamental: **conservation must work for the people who live closest to wildlife.**

## Listening to the people of Nubi

Last week, I had the opportunity to accompany WWF Bhutan to **Nubi Gewog** to discuss an innovative approach to conservation financing: the development of a **Tiger Impact Certificate for Nubi**.

The concept is a results-based conservation financing approach designed to recognize and reward communities for verified tiger conservation and human–tiger coexistence outcomes, with benefits flowing back to local development and conservation actions.

Nubi Gewog lies within **Bhutan's Biological Corridor 8**, an important landscape linking Wangchuck Centennial National Park and Jigme Singye Wangchuck National Park.

This corridor is much more than a line on a map. It is a living passage for wildlife and a landscape where people, livestock, forests and tigers share the same space.

Nubi is also part of DFO Bumthang, known for having one of the largest tiger numbers outside Bhutan's protected areas. Studies toward the northern side of Nubi have recorded numerous tigers cohabiting with communities, highlighting the ecological importance of this landscape.

## The real cost of coexistence

![Forestry and WWF officials inspecting a livestock kill](/assets/news/nubi-tiger-coexistence/tiger-livestock-kill.jpg)
*Forestry and WWF officials inspecting a milking cow killed by a tiger.*

Behind this conservation success are the everyday realities faced by local people.

Livestock depredation is not a rare event in Nubi; for some households, it can be a weekly or even daily concern. Cattle are not simply economic assets. They are part of rural livelihoods, household security and cultural life.

When a tiger kills a milking cow, the loss is deeply felt. Any conservation approach must therefore acknowledge this reality with empathy and practical support for the people who bear the costs of living with wildlife.

## Acceptance should never be taken for granted

![Community consultation in Nubi Gewog](/assets/news/nubi-tiger-coexistence/community-consultation.jpg)
*Results from the community consultation on the Tiger Impact Certificate with participants from Bji-Semji Chiwog of Nubi Gewog.*

What inspired me most during the consultation was the attitude of the people of Nubi.

Despite the negative consequences of living with tigers, most participants expressed no negative feelings toward the animal. Many still believe that **tigers must remain in the ecosystem**.

At the same time, community members shared thoughtful ideas for improving cohabitation and coexistence. Their views demonstrated something important: acceptance already exists, but **it should never be taken for granted**.

## Investing in coexistence

Innovations such as the Tiger Impact Certificate are necessary and timely. They can help sustain the longstanding bond between people and tigers while ensuring that communities are not left alone to carry the costs of a conservation success that has significance far beyond their villages.

I hope to see more such ideas from conservationists, philanthropists, government agencies and private entities to strengthen coexistence—or simply to help maintain the remarkable acceptance already shown by communities like Nubi.

## Returning to where it began

Nubi has always been close to my heart.

A decade ago, I began my career with **Wangchuck Centennial National Park**, and my first visit took me to Mangdiphu village in Nubi Gewog, where I saw tiger pugmarks for the first time.

Returning now, I see not only signs of tigers, but also **signs of hope**: communities willing to coexist, partners willing to innovate, and a landscape that continues to connect Bhutan's conservation future.
