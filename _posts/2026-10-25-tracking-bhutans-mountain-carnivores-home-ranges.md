*Reflections from my PhD fieldwork and movement-ecology research in Bhutan.*

What does it take to understand how a carnivore moves through Bhutan's steep, densely forested mountains? Camera traps can tell us when an animal passes a particular location. But following its movements over weeks and months is a different challenge altogether. During my PhD research, working alongside dedicated forest rangers and wildlife professionals, I learned how difficult—and how rewarding—it can be to safely capture, collar and track some of Bhutan's most elusive carnivores.

## Humane capture in difficult mountain terrain

The work took place in rugged forest landscapes, including areas of Jigme Singye Wangchuck National Park and Royal Manas National Park. We had to identify suitable sites, transport and establish live traps, revisit equipment in challenging terrain, and respond promptly whenever a trap was triggered. We also depended on careful coordination among trained rangers, wildlife-handling personnel and veterinary professionals.

Animal welfare was central to the work. Live traps were camouflaged with vegetation and equipped with electronic door alarms that sent alerts when traps closed. In my thesis, I report around-the-clock remote trap monitoring, with a 99% monitoring success rate, intended to minimise the time animals spent confined. Capture, restraint and collaring procedures followed approved protocols and involved trained personnel.

## How much space do mountain carnivores need?

To estimate the area used by each tracked animal, I analysed GPS and VHF telemetry data with an **autocorrelated kernel density estimation (AKDE)** framework. Unlike a simple boundary around locations, this approach accounts for the fact that successive animal positions are related in time.

The findings showed substantial variation not only among species, but also among individuals of the same species. The figures below are **95% home-range estimates for tracked individuals**, not universal home-range sizes for every animal of their species.

![Comparison of estimated home-range areas and confidence intervals](/assets/news/bhutan-carnivore-tracking/home-range-comparison.png)
*Home-range estimates from Table 4.1 of my PhD thesis, using weighted AKDE (km²). Error bars show 95% confidence intervals. Locations of animal movements are deliberately not shown.*

### Common leopard: striking variation among individuals

Three common leopards illustrated how dramatically ranging behaviour can differ. The tracked female had an estimated home range of **14.61 km²**, while a male tracked in Royal Manas National Park used **72.23 km²**. A second male tracked in western Bhutan ranged over **531.59 km²**. Differences in landscape, resources and individual behaviour may help explain this variation, and results from a few animals should not be treated as species-wide averages.

### Asiatic golden cat and leopard cat: smaller cats, substantial ranging needs

The tracked **female Asiatic golden cat** had an estimated home range of **32.21 km²**. The **male leopard cat** had a very similar estimate of **30.10 km²**. Even relatively small forest cats can range across substantial areas in search of food, shelter and other resources.

### Dhole: a social hunter, very different ranges

The two tracked **female dholes** also differed markedly. One tracked in western Bhutan had an estimated home range of **57.24 km²**, whereas the individual tracked in Jigme Singye Wangchuck National Park had an estimated range of **359.44 km²**. My thesis discusses potential influences of habitat and pack characteristics on this contrast, but these explanations remain interpretations rather than definitive causes.

## What the tracking taught us

The tracking data helped reveal how mountain carnivores respond differently to elevation, forest cover, human settlements, rivers, prey and other carnivores. One clear lesson was that **there is no single home-range figure that captures the lives of all carnivores, or even all individuals of one species**. Space use is shaped by individual circumstances and the landscapes through which animals move.

This work also reinforced the need to pair technological advances with rigorous fieldcraft, animal welfare and conservation judgement. A GPS collar produces useful locations only because field teams first undertake difficult, careful and humane work in the mountains. The findings can help improve wildlife monitoring, habitat management and conservation planning—while reminding us how much remains to be learned about Bhutan's forest carnivores.
