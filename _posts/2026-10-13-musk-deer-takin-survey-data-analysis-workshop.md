From **27 September to 2 October 2026**, the **Department of Forests and Park Services (DoFPS)** convened a six-day **Musk Deer and Takin Survey Data Analysis and Report Generation Workshop in Mongar**. The workshop was an unprecedented undertaking in its analytical scope: data management, analysis and technical reporting focused not only on the two target species, **musk deer and takin**, but also on other wildlife captured during the camera-trap surveys.

## Beyond Musk Deer and Takin

Although the national surveys were designed to assess musk deer and takin, the camera traps also recorded a much wider range of mammals, including **other ungulates, wild felids, carnivores and small mammals**. The Mongar workshop recognised the conservation value of these additional records and brought them into an integrated data management and analysis process.

By analysing the wider camera-trap dataset, DoFPS aims to generate knowledge about mammal occurrence, site use, habitat relationships and, where appropriate, activity patterns. This approach helps maximise the scientific return from an extensive and demanding national field effort.

## Putting Field Knowledge at the Centre of Analysis

A defining feature of the workshop was the involvement of **field personnel with first-hand experience of the survey** in data analysis and report preparation. Their understanding of camera deployment, species identification, field conditions and local ecology was essential for interpreting the statistical results in their proper conservation context.

The workshop brought field expertise and analytical skills into the same working environment, encouraging careful verification of records, systematic organisation of camera-station information, interpretation of model outputs and preparation of technical reports.

## From Camera-Trap Records to Scientific Evidence

The wider analytical work illustrates the potential of a multi-species approach. The supplied workshop materials include **Bayesian site-use effects** for barking deer, Himalayan goral, Himalayan serow, sambar and wild pig, with modelled relationships involving covariates such as distance to roads, elevation, slope and temperature. Rather than treating these species as incidental records, such analyses extract additional ecological information from the national survey.

A further example is a **modelled occupancy-probability map for yellow-throated marten**, demonstrating how camera-trap records can support spatially explicit wildlife assessments. The workshop materials also feature a technical report titled *Occupancy, Detection and Activity Patterns of Wild Cats in Bhutan: Evidence from Camera-trap By-catch Data, 2025–2026*. Together, these outputs underline the breadth of biodiversity evidence that can be developed from carefully managed survey data.

The figures and maps are examples of analytical outputs; they should not be interpreted as final published musk deer or takin density estimates.

## Scientific Caution and Protection of Sensitive Species Data

The central objective remains the finalisation of the **national musk deer and takin survey report**, which is expected to present density estimates supported by the survey data and suitable scientific methods.

Such estimates require careful evaluation of statistical assumptions, uncertainty and limitations. The Department is approaching the reporting process cautiously to ensure that findings are scientifically defensible and that conservation decisions rest on credible evidence.

Equally important is the **integrity, security and confidentiality of sensitive wildlife data**. Precise locations and potentially sensitive distribution information for threatened species should not be disclosed through public maps or communications where doing so could place wildlife at risk. Public results can instead present appropriate summaries while protecting underlying survey records.

## A Milestone for Integrated Biodiversity Monitoring

The Mongar workshop represents more than a report-writing exercise. It demonstrates the importance of combining **field expertise, data quality assurance, quantitative analysis and scientific reporting** to move from individual camera-trap detections to meaningful conservation information.

By extending the analysis to ungulates, felids, carnivores and small mammals, DoFPS has shown how a survey established for two focal species can contribute to a wider picture of Bhutan's biodiversity. It is a valuable step toward more integrated and evidence-based national wildlife monitoring.

The **final Musk Deer and Takin Survey Report will be launched soon**. Its findings will be communicated with scientific care, appropriate treatment of uncertainty and necessary safeguards for the threatened species the survey seeks to conserve.
